THE DEEP SPACE ARCHIVE · 1972–2003

Pioneer 10

The first spacecraft to fly past Jupiter, cross the asteroid belt and set out beyond the outer planets.

Pioneer 10 spacecraft · NASA Ames Research Center

01 — The Beginning

Pioneer 10 was an uncrewed NASA spacecraft launched in March 1972. Its main goal was to make the first close-up study of Jupiter. It was built to investigate the giant planet and its environment, while testing whether a spacecraft could safely travel through the asteroid belt and the intense radiation near Jupiter.

04 — What Did It Help Us Discover?

Pioneer 10 returned the first close-up images of Jupiter and measured its atmosphere, magnetic field, magnetosphere and radiation environment. These observations helped scientists understand the giant planet and informed the design of later missions that travelled to Jupiter, including Voyager and Galileo.

05 — Why Was It Left Behind?

Pioneer 10 was never intended to land or return to Earth. After completing its Jupiter encounter, it continued outward through the outer solar system. Its radioisotope power source gradually produced less electricity, eventually leaving too little power for its transmitter to send a detectable signal.

06 — Where Is It Now?

Pioneer 10 is no longer communicating with Earth. Its last faint signal was received on January 23, 2003, when it was about 7.6 billion miles (12.23 billion kilometres) away. It continues to coast silently along its trajectory.

07 — Its Legacy

Pioneer 10 opened the way for exploration of the outer planets. It also carried a plaque with diagrams intended to communicate information about humanity and Earth's location if the spacecraft were ever found by an intelligent civilization.
